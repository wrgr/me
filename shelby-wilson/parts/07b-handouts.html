The same idea, told three ways

One story for a classroom, one for a seminar room, one for a stranger. Each ends with something a group can actually do. These print one to a page — use your browser’s print dialog and everything else on this page drops away.

For kids · ages 9–13

The class average doesn’t have a favourite colour

Nobody in your class is 11.4 years old with 1.8 pets.

If you add up everyone’s age and divide, you get a number that describes the class and belongs to nobody. That’s an average. Averages are useful — but they delete people.

Shelby Wilson is a mathematician who spends her whole career refusing to let that happen. When she studied cancer drugs, she didn’t build one model for “a mouse” — she built a model for each of 105 mice. When she studied grooming in animal groups, she found something surprising: the group with the three most popular animals — the ones everybody groomed — was the group that stayed sickest. The group where everyone groomed randomly got better.

Group activity — the vanishing classroom

  1. Everyone writes down three numbers about themselves: height in cm, hours slept last night, number of letters in their first name.
  2. Work out the class average for each. Write “The Average Student” on the board with those three numbers.
  3. Now go round the room: does anyone match all three? Usually nobody does.
  4. Plot everyone’s three numbers as dots. Draw the average as a big dot in the middle.
  5. Discuss: if you had to buy one chair for The Average Student, would it fit anybody? When is an average a good idea — and when is it a way of not looking?

For peers · seminar

One estimator, six substrates

The claim: her career is a single methodological commitment applied at ascending scales.

Wilson’s work is usually described by domain — immunology, oncology, sleep, behavioural ecology, epidemic forecasting. Sorted that way it reads as itinerant. Sorted by what unit carries the random effect, it is monotone: a cell (2010–13), a mouse (2013–15), a sleeper (2015–20), an animal in a troop (2018–20), a model (2021–24), a mathematician (2016–).

The load-bearing observation is that the mixed-effect model and the linear opinion pool are the same commitment run in opposite directions. One decomposes a population into shared plus individual structure; the other composes individuals into a population without collapsing their variance. Both refuse the point estimate. Both appear in her record, twelve years apart, in fields that do not cite each other.

Group activity — the isomorphism test

  1. Take three of her papers that look least related — say the 2013 Treg switching paper, the 2020 allogrooming paper, and the 2023 Scenario Modeling Hub evaluation.
  2. Write each on a card in three slots only: what is the population · what is the individual · what is being refused.
  3. Compare cards. If the slots fill the same way across unrelated fields, that is a real structural claim rather than a nice sentence.
  4. Then adversarially: find the paper that breaks it. Argue for the NTRU undergraduate thesis, or the multi-scale VEGF model, as genuine counterexamples.
  5. Repeat the exercise on a researcher in your own department whose CV looks scattered. Does an axis exist that makes it monotone?

For the public

Why the pandemic forecast was a committee

During COVID-19 the number you saw on the news was not one scientist’s guess. It was dozens, deliberately kept apart and then added up.

More than ninety research groups submitted forecasts to a public archive every week. Every submission was timestamped and kept, including the wrong ones. When researchers later scored them, the combination of all those forecasts was more consistently accurate than any single group — for eighteen months straight.

Two things are worth knowing. First: the combination works because it keeps the disagreement. The width of the shaded band on those charts is how much the modellers disagreed, and it is information, not embarrassment. Second: they published their failures. When forecasts of new cases were wrong, they said how wrong — during three big waves, ranges advertised as right 19 times out of 20 were right fewer than 1 time in 2.

Group activity — the jar and the crowd

  1. Fill a jar with sweets, marbles, anything countable. Only one person knows the true number.
  2. Everyone writes a private guess and a range they’re 90% sure contains the answer. No discussion.
  3. Collect all guesses. Compute the median. Compare it with each individual guess.
  4. Count how many people’s 90% ranges actually contained the truth. If far fewer than 90% did, you have just reproduced the paper’s central finding: people’s ranges are too narrow.
  5. Now let everyone talk for two minutes and guess again. Does the crowd get better — or just more confident? That difference is the whole problem.
