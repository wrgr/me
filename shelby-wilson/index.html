The individual inside the population

Nineteen papers across immunology, cancer pharmacology, sleep, animal social behaviour and pandemic forecasting — plus a website about Black mathematicians. It reads like six careers. It is one problem, restated at six scales.

Every model she has ever built is a machine for refusing to let the average stand in for its members.

This page is an interpretation of a real person's real work, assembled from public sources. The argument is mine; the evidence is hers, and every claim below links to where it came from.

The theory

Three laws the bibliography obeys

A through-line is only interesting if it could be wrong. Each of these is falsifiable against her publication record: find a paper that breaks it and the law fails.

Law I

No average is allowed to stand in for its members.

The population curve is never the answer; it is the thing you subtract to find the individual. Her method of choice — mixed-effect modelling — is literally an equation with a private parameter set for every subject bolted onto a shared one.

Evidence 1,371 tumour measurements across 105 mice, fitted with a mixed-effect approach — Wilson et al., CPT:PSP 2015. Her INRIA postdoc title names the move outright: “…Yields Individualized Predictions for Optimal Combined Cancer Treatment”.

Law II

Population structure is an outcome, not an input.

She refuses to assume the shape of the group. The organisation is what you get out of the model after individuals follow local rules — which means it can be surprising, and repeatedly is.

Evidence The 2018 chapter titles say it: “…the Evolution of Social Behaviors and Emergent Population Organization”. In Wilson et al., Front. Ecol. Evol. 2020, the same 50 animals self-organise into three hubs groomed by 49 others each — or into nobody in particular — purely by changing who they choose.

Law III

Combine, don’t select — and keep the disagreement.

Given many imperfect individuals, do not pick the best one. Pool them in a way that preserves how much they differ. The spread is not noise around the answer; it is part of the answer.

Evidence An ensemble that preserved variation between models (linear opinion pool) beat every single model — Nat. Commun. 2023. APL’s own submission, JHUAPL-SLPHospEns, is flagged in the hub metadata as an ensemble_of_hub_models — a model made of other people’s models.

The paper that doesn’t fit

Her Spelman undergraduate thesis was on the NTRU public key cryptosystem — lattice cryptography, with no population anywhere in it. It is the one item in the record that the theory cannot absorb, and it is worth saying so plainly. It is also the last problem she worked on before she got to choose her own.

The story

Six rungs, one move

Read in spine order rather than date order, the record stops looking like someone changing fields and starts looking like someone taking the same question up a ladder, testing whether it still holds at the next size.

Rung one

a cell

T cells, regulatory T cells, a tumour

2010 · 2012 · 2013

She starts with a population that argues with itself

Her doctoral work under Doron Levy at Maryland is about the immune system, and specifically about the awkward fact that an immune response has to know when to stop. Her first publication, a 2010 conference paper, proposes that the stopping is done by a subpopulation of regulatory T cells that adapts to the response it is watching.

The 2012 paper that followed is her most-cited work by a wide margin. Wilson and Levy build a four-equation model — tumour, TGF-β, effector cells, regulatory cells — to explain why blocking TGF-β does nearly nothing alone but transforms the effect of a tumour vaccine. The model reproduces the experiment it was fitted to, which was the point: it was built to be checkable against Terabe et al.’s mice.

Then, in 2013, the move that turns out to be the whole career. The biology was in conflict: some evidence said regulatory T cells could stop regulating and start promoting, some said they could not. She did not pick a side and she did not average the two. She made the switching itself a parameter, modelled the population as containing cells that could change what they were, and found that this made the immune response dramatically less sensitive to how many cells you assumed at the start.

The move: when a population is internally inconsistent, do not resolve the inconsistency by averaging. Model the thing that lets individuals differ.

Rung two

a mouse

xenografted animals on a dosing schedule

2013 · 2015

At INRIA she learns the equation for the whole idea

After the PhD she goes to INRIA Grenoble, to Benjamin Ribba’s group, and the subject changes from immunology to pharmacology: when, exactly, should you give chemotherapy relative to a drug that is busy remodelling the tumour’s blood supply? The 2013 multi-scale model answers it by inventing something to measure — an index of vasculature quality, the balance of stable to unstable vessels — so that “wait for the vessels to normalise” becomes a number you can optimise against.

But the paper that matters here is the 2015 scheduling study, and it matters because of its method. 1,371 tumour measurements across 105 colorectal tumour-bearing mice, fitted with a mixed-effect approach: one population model, plus a private parameter set for every single animal. The title of the postdoc project she was hired for says it outright — “The Stochastic Approximation of Expectation Maximization Yields Individualized Predictions for Optimal Combined Cancer Treatment.”

This is Law I with an equation attached. A mixed-effect model is, structurally, a formal apparatus for saying the average is not the subject: you fit the population and the deviation from it at the same time, and the deviations are data, not error.

Rung three

a sleeper

one body regulating its own temperature

2015 · 2020 · (talks, 2015–2019)

The individual becomes a whole organism, and gains a room

The sleep work comes out of a long-running collaboration with five other mathematicians — Bañuelos, Best, Huguet, Prieto-Langarica, Pyzza — and it makes the same move one level up. A night’s sleep is not one state; it is a population of interacting neuronal subcircuits. The 2015 chapter splits sleep into REM and NREM populations so that the architecture of a night emerges from switching between them rather than being written in by hand.

The 2020 paper then does something quietly radical for a sleep model: it puts the room in the equations. Following the theory that REM sleep is when the body stops paying the metabolic bill for thermoregulation, the model adds heat-balance equations to the circuit and asks what an ambient temperature away from thermoneutral does to the shape of a night — and then of several nights running. Their conclusion is blunt: including temperature “is a vital component of modeling sleep.”

There is a fourth thread on this rung that never became a paper. Between 2015 and 2019 she gave talks on the dynamics of coupled Morris–Lecar neurons at Colorado, at Georgia, at a SIAM meeting, at SACNAS, and as the Dorothy Wrinch Lecture in Biomathematics. None of it is in the publication record. A bibliography is itself a population summary, and it loses individuals.

Rung four

an animal in a troop

who grooms whom, and who benefits

2018 · 2018 · 2020

The most surprising result she has published

In March 2017, at a Mathematical Biosciences Institute workshop, eight women — Brooks, Hohn, Price, Radunskaya, Sindi, Williams, Wilson and Nina Fefferman — started a project on ectoparasites and allogrooming. It produced two chapters in 2018 and a paper in 2020 on which Wilson is first author.

The setup: fifty animals, each grooming five neighbours, dropping two partners and picking two new ones every step for 300 steps. Nothing varies but the rule for choosing partners — at random, or by preferring the most connected, the most central, or the best-bridging animals. Then parasites are introduced.

Choose partners by popularity and the group organises itself into three hub animals groomed by 49 others each, with everyone else averaging fewer than five groomers — a picture of a dominance hierarchy that arises without anyone imposing it. That population stays saturated with parasites at every grooming efficiency tested. Choose partners at random, and if grooming is efficient enough, the outbreak dies out entirely.

The finding: the most organised society is the sickest one. The authors’ reading is that allogrooming is probably about social bonding, and has to overcome a parasite penalty to pay for itself — not the other way round.

And the sting in the discussion, which reads differently after 2020: fighting an endemic parasite “requires a fundamentally different social structure than preventing the spread of a novel invasive threat.” A population optimised against the disease it already has is the population most exposed to the one it has not met.

Rung five

a model

one team’s forecast among dozens

2021 · 2022 ×3 · 2023 ×3 · 2024

Where the individual is somebody else’s model

She joined the Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory in 2020, and the rung changes again: now the population is a population of models, and the individual whose difference must be preserved is another team’s SEIR code. Eight papers in four years, with author counts of 60, 63, 83, 109, 295 and 428 — numbers that are themselves the argument.

The PNAS evaluation scored tens of millions of predictions from more than 90 groups: the multi-model ensemble was the most consistently accurate forecaster of COVID-19 deaths for eighteen months, while two-thirds of individual models merely beat a naïve baseline. The Nature Communications evaluation of the Scenario Modeling Hub graded nearly 1.8 million projections and found that scenarios stayed close to reality for 22 weeks on average before an unanticipated variant broke their assumptions — and that the ensemble which preserved variation between models was consistently more reliable than any single one.

The pooling method has a name — the linear opinion pool — and it does precisely what a mixed-effect model does, in the other direction: it keeps the members distinguishable inside the aggregate. APL’s own entry to the Forecast Hub, JHUAPL-SLPHospEns, is flagged in the hub dataset as an ensemble_of_hub_models: a model whose members are other people’s models.

What makes this thread trustworthy rather than triumphal is how much of it is published failure. The Delta paper says in its own abstract that the models got the surge and its timing right and the magnitude “substantially underestimated.” The 2024 case-forecasting review reports that during the growth phases of the winter 2020, Delta and Omicron waves, coverage of the 95% prediction intervals fell below 50% — a forecast advertised as right nineteen times in twenty being right fewer than one time in two.

Rung six

a mathematician

a named person, not a demographic

2016 · 2017– · 2018 · 2019

The same operation, performed on her own field

In 2017, with Erica Graham, Raegan Higgins and Candice Price, she launched Mathematically Gifted and Black, and wrote it up the next year in the Notices of the AMS. The site publishes one Black mathematician per day of Black History Month, each answering the same handful of questions in their own words.

It is easy to file this under outreach and move on. Read it against the rest of the record and it is the same technique. A diversity statistic is a population average; it tells you a proportion and erases every member. MGB replaces the statistic with the individuals — named, photographed, quoted, each with their own parameters. That is the linear opinion pool applied to people: combine, but do not average away.

The same instinct runs through the rest of this rung — co-founding the Network of Mathematicians of Color and CodeHouse, co-organising AIM workshops, a chapter on working with students from underrepresented groups, and a list of named undergraduates in her CV whose projects she supervised, one of whom is a Rhodes Scholar and several of whom went on to graduate school. She keeps the names.

Plates I – XIX

Every publication, drawn and explained

One illustration per paper, drawn from what that paper actually does rather than from its field. Numbers are quoted from the papers themselves; anything I could not verify is marked as such rather than smoothed over.

Plate I 2010 · a cell

A Mathematical Model of the Primary T Cell Response with Contraction Governed by Adaptive Regulatory T Cells

S. N. Wilson, P. Lee, D. Levy · IFMBE Proceedings 32:209–212 · 10.1007/978-3-642-14998-6_53

Her first publication, from the Southern Biomedical Engineering Conference held at her own university. An immune response has three acts — it expands, it contracts, it leaves memory behind — and the question is what performs act two. The proposal is that contraction is not decay but regulation: a subpopulation that adapts to the response it is suppressing.

  • 3 authors, including Peter Lee at Stanford and her advisor Doron Levy
  • 1 citation on Crossref — and it is the seed of the two papers that follow

Plate II 2012 · a cell

A Mathematical Model of the Enhancement of Tumor Vaccine Efficacy by Immunotherapy

S. Wilson, D. Levy · Bulletin of Mathematical Biology 74(7) · 10.1007/s11538-012-9722-4

Her most-cited paper. TGF-β is an immunoregulatory protein that helps tumours evade the immune system; blocking it alone does little. The model — tumour size, TGF-β concentration, activated cytotoxic effector cells and regulatory T cells — was built to reproduce a specific mouse experiment, and does, including the part where only the combination eradicates the tumour.

  • 4 coupled state variables; solved in MATLAB R2010a with ode23
  • Tumour growth rate 0.1946 day⁻¹ and carrying capacity 369 mm², both fitted to the control data of Terabe et al. (2009)
  • Vaccine modelled as 5,000 TGF-β-resistant effector cells added on day 3; simulations run day 3 → day 30
  • Initial condition assumes ~100 activated effector cells at the tumour site
  • 88 citations — more than the rest of her pre-2020 work combined

Plate III 2013 · a cell

Functional Switching and Stability of Regulatory T Cells

S. Wilson, D. Levy · Bulletin of Mathematical Biology 75(10) · 10.1007/s11538-013-9875-9

The pivotal small paper. The immunology literature disagreed about whether regulatory T cells can lose their regulatory function and become immunostimulatory. Rather than assume an answer, she made the transition a modelled mechanism — in an ODE version and in a more biologically detailed delay-differential version — and asked what it buys you.

  • Models with switching still show the normal expansion, contraction and memory phases, but are more robust to the assumed precursor frequency of T cells
  • The paper identifies a biologically testable range for the switching parameter — a modelling result posed as an experiment
  • Conclusion: regulatory T cell switching “may play a key role in controlling immune contraction”

Plate IV 2013 · a mouse

Theoretical investigation of the efficacy of antiangiogenic drugs combined to chemotherapy in xenografted mice

F. Lignet, S. Benzekry, S. Wilson, F. Billy, O. Saut, M. Tod, B. You et al. · Journal of Theoretical Biology 320 · 10.1016/j.jtbi.2012.12.013

A multi-scale model that couples a molecular description of VEGF signalling to a tissue-scale description of tumour growth. Its contribution is a definition: an index of vasculature quality, set by the balance of stable to unstable vessels inside the tumour mass, which converts a vague clinical intuition into an optimisation target.

  • 12 authors across the INRIA, Bordeaux and Lyon groups
  • The question posed is explicitly one of timing: how long to wait after the antiangiogenic before delivering chemotherapy
  • Framed as groundwork for translating preclinical scheduling into clinical studies

Plate V 2015 · a mouse · the method keystone

Modeling and predicting optimal treatment scheduling between the antiangiogenic drug sunitinib and irinotecan in preclinical settings

S. Wilson, M. Tod, A. Ouerdani, A. Emde, Y. Yarden, B. Ribba et al. · CPT: Pharmacometrics & Systems Pharmacology 4(12) · 10.1002/psp4.12045

The paper where the method and the philosophy are the same object. A system of nonlinear ODEs for tumour growth, vasculature generation and antiangiogenic treatment, fitted to a large longitudinal mouse dataset — and then, crucially, validated against a follow-up experiment it had not seen.

  • 1,371 longitudinal tumour size measurements in 105 colorectal tumour-bearing mice
  • Parameters estimated “either using a mixed-effect approach or by nonlinear least squares”, depending on the dataset
  • A log-likelihood ratio test supports a synergistic interaction between sunitinib and irinotecan
  • Simulations were then compared against a follow-up preclinical experiment — the model had predictive value for the timing window, not just descriptive fit
  • Note: her CV lists this under an earlier title (“…combination scheduling between antiangiogenic drug and chemotherapy”); the published title names the two drugs. The published version is used here.

Plate VI 2015 · a sleeper

Effects of Thermoregulation on Human Sleep Patterns: A Mathematical Model of Sleep–Wake Cycles with REM–NREM Subcircuit

S. Bañuelos, J. Best, G. Huguet, A. Prieto-Langarica, P. B. Pyzza, M. H. Schmidt, S. Wilson · The IMA Volumes in Mathematics and its Applications 158 · 10.1007/978-1-4939-2782-1_6

The first of the sleep papers, from a collaboration of six mathematicians that would keep going for five years. Existing sleep–wake models treated sleep as a single state. This one adds the REM/NREM subcircuit, so that the internal architecture of a night is produced by the model rather than assumed by it.

  • 7 authors, including sleep physiologist Markus H. Schmidt — the domain expert inside a mathematics collaboration
  • Published in an IMA volume on Applications of Dynamical Systems in Biology and Medicine

Plate VII 2020 · a sleeper

Modeling the long term effects of thermoregulation on human sleep

S. Bañuelos, J. Best, G. Huguet, A. Prieto-Langarica, P. B. Pyzza, S. Wilson · Journal of Theoretical Biology 494:110208 · 10.1016/j.jtbi.2020.110208

The follow-up, five years later, extends the model with equations for the metabolic processes controlling thermoregulation during sleep — motivated by a theory that REM sleep is precisely when energy normally spent on temperature control is diverted elsewhere. The model then answers, quantitatively, how a sleeper adjusts their stage progression when the room is not thermoneutral.

  • Simulations cover both a single night and several consecutive nights — the “long term” of the title
  • Stated conclusion: “including the effects of temperature is a vital component of modeling sleep”
  • The illustration’s fragmentation pattern is schematic — it shows the paper’s claim, not digitised output. Per-stage durations were not extracted; the full text is behind a paywall.

Plate VIII 2018 · an animal in a troop

How Disease Risks Can Impact the Evolution of Social Behaviors and Emergent Population Organization

N. D. Williams, H. Z. Brooks, M. E. Hohn, C. R. Price, A. E. Radunskaya, S. S. Sindi, S. N. Wilson, N. H. Fefferman · Association for Women in Mathematics Series 14, 31–46 · 10.1007/978-3-319-98083-6_2

The first output of the workshop project. If being social means being exposed, then infection pressure ought to be one of the forces that shaped animal societies in the first place — which reframes social structure from a fixed setting into an evolved outcome.

  • 8 authors, all women, from a Mathematical Biosciences Institute Women Advancing Mathematical Biology workshop (March 2017) where Wilson was a group co-lead
  • Published in the Understanding Complex Biological Systems with Mathematics volume of the AWM Series
  • 3 citations — the ideas travelled through the 2020 paper instead

Plate IX 2018 · an animal in a troop

Mathematical Analysis of the Impact of Social Structure on Ectoparasite Load in Allogrooming Populations

H. Z. Brooks, M. E. Hohn, C. R. Price, A. E. Radunskaya, S. S. Sindi, N. D. Williams, S. N. Wilson, N. H. Fefferman · Association for Women in Mathematics Series 14, 47–61 · 10.1007/978-3-319-98083-6_3

The companion chapter, running the comparison the other way: fix the grooming behaviour, vary the social structure, and see where the parasite burden ends up. It is the analysis that the 2020 paper turns into a full simulation study.

  • Same 8 authors, adjacent pages in the same volume — the pair is really one piece of work
  • 0 Crossref citations. It is a load-bearing step in the argument that nobody has cited, which is the ordinary fate of chapters
  • The bar heights above are schematic, taking their ordering from the 2020 paper’s published results. The chapter’s own figures were not accessible.

Plate X 2020 · an animal in a troop

How Emergent Social Patterns in Allogrooming Combat Parasitic Infections

S. N. Wilson, S. S. Sindi, H. Z. Brooks, M. E. Hohn, C. R. Price, A. E. Radunskaya, N. D. Williams, N. H. Fefferman · Frontiers in Ecology and Evolution 8:54 · 10.3389/fevo.2020.00054

Wilson is first author. Fifty animals, each grooming five neighbours; every step they drop their two lowest-ranked partners and pick two new ones. Grooming both removes parasites from the groomee and moves parasites along the edge. The only thing that varies between conditions is the rule for choosing partners.

  • N = 50 hosts, 5 connections each, 2 partners swapped per step, 300 steps, 100 trials per condition, in MATLAB R2019a
  • Network organises for the first 150 steps; parasites are introduced to a single host at step 150
  • Under degree-preference, exactly 3 core nodes end up with 49 groomers each; every other node averages fewer than 5
  • Grooming efficiencies tested: g = 0, 0.001, 0.004, 0.008. At g = 0.004 only the random-affiliation population extinguishes the outbreak
  • Other parameters: max load m = 1, reproduction factor r = 0.01, bidirectional spread factor α = 0.15, transmission taken as 10% of a host’s neighbours’ average load
  • Discussion: fighting an endemic parasite “requires a fundamentally different social structure than preventing the spread of a novel invasive threat”

Plate XI 2021 · a model

Modeling of Future COVID-19 Cases, Hospitalizations, and Deaths, by Vaccination Rates and Nonpharmaceutical Intervention Scenarios — United States, April–September 2021

R. K. Borchering, C. Viboud, E. Howerton and 57 others including S. Wilson · MMWR 70(19) · 10.15585/mmwr.mm7019e3

Her first COVID-19 paper, a year into the job, published in the CDC’s own bulletin. Six models projected six months forward across four scenarios, using data available through 27 March 2021.

  • 6 models, 4 scenarios, a 6-month horizon (April–September 2021)
  • Key finding is an interaction, not a main effect: accelerated decline in adherence to precautions “was shown to undermine vaccination-related gains over the subsequent 2–3 months”
  • 148 citations — the most-cited item in her record after the PNAS evaluation

Plate XII 2022 · a model

Projected resurgence of COVID-19 in the United States in July–December 2021 resulting from the increased transmissibility of the Delta variant and faltering vaccination

S. Truelove, C. P. Smith, M. Qin and 57 others including S. Wilson · eLife 11:e73584 · 10.7554/eLife.73584

An ensemble of nine mechanistic models projected six months ahead for the Delta wave. Published a year later, with the answer known, the paper grades itself in public — and the grade is mixed.

  • 9 mechanistic models, 6-month scenario projections for July–December 2021
  • Reaching higher vaccination coverage was estimated to avert over 1.5 million cases and 21,000 deaths
  • Impact concentrated in a subset of states with lower vaccination coverage
  • Stated in the abstract: “the magnitude was substantially underestimated … by the models compared with the … reported cases, hospitalizations, and deaths”
  • Licence CC0 — the authors placed it in the public domain

Plate XIII 2022 · a model · the Law III keystone

Evaluation of individual and ensemble probabilistic forecasts of COVID-19 mortality in the United States

E. Y. Cramer, E. L. Ray, V. K. Lopez and 292 others including S. Wilson · PNAS 119(15) · 10.1073/pnas.2113561119

Her most-cited paper, and the clearest statement of Law III in the record. The US COVID-19 Forecast Hub collected tens of millions of predictions from more than 90 academic, industry and independent groups; this paper scores them.

  • The multi-model ensemble gave “the most consistently accurate probabilistic forecasts of incident deaths” from April 2020 through October 2021 — 18 months
  • 27 individual models submitted complete forecasts throughout; two-thirds beat a naïve baseline
  • Probabilistic error at a 20-week horizon was 3–5× larger than at 1 week
  • 295 authors · 315 Crossref citations

Plate XIV 2022 · a model

The United States COVID-19 Forecast Hub dataset

E. Y. Cramer, Y. Huang, Y. Wang and 425 others including S. Wilson · Scientific Data 9:462 · 10.1038/s41597-022-01517-w

The infrastructure paper — a data descriptor rather than a result. It is included here because it is the physical embodiment of the theory: the hub keeps every team’s predictions separately and permanently, which is what makes both the ensemble and the honest scoring possible.

  • 428 authors — the largest author list in her record
  • Names the models that are themselves combinations of hub models, flagged with the metadata field ensemble_of_hub_models: FDANIHASU-Sweight, JHUAPL-SLPHospEns, KITmetricslab-select_ensemble
  • APL’s mechanistic entry, JHUAPL-Bucky, was funded by the US Department of Health and Human Services
  • Ships with an R package, covidHubUtils, so the scoring is reproducible by anyone

Plate XV 2023 · a model

Impact of SARS-CoV-2 vaccination of children ages 5–11 years on COVID-19 disease burden and resilience to new variants in the United States, November 2021–March 2022: a multi-model study

R. K. Borchering, L. C. Mullany, E. Howerton and 60 others including S. Wilson · The Lancet Regional Health – Americas 17:100398 · 10.1016/j.lana.2022.100398

A single policy question — should 5-to-11-year-olds be vaccinated — answered by nine teams independently, then pooled linearly and combined by meta-analysis. This is the hub used for a decision rather than a post-mortem.

  • All-age cumulative cases −7.2% (mean IR 0.928, 95% CI 0.880–0.977)
  • Hospitalisations −8.7% (mean IR 0.913, 95% CI 0.834–0.992)
  • Deaths −9.2% (mean IR 0.908, 95% CI 0.797–1.020) — note the interval crosses 1
  • Relative reductions were larger for children than for the whole population, and benefits grew under the more-transmissible-variant scenario
  • Scenarios were defined before Omicron emerged — the paper says so, and it is why the projections need reading as conditional

Plate XVI 2023 · a model · the keystone

On the Mark: Modeling and Forecasting for Public Health Impact

M. J. Panaggio, S. N. Wilson, J. D. Ratcliff, L. C. Mullany, J. D. Freeman, K. Rainwater-Lovett · Health Security 21(S1):S79–S88 · 10.1089/hs.2023.0033

Six authors, all from the APL group she works in, and she is second. It is the only paper in the record that argues about what modelling and forecasting are for rather than performing some — which is exactly the profile of a keystone paper, and exactly why nobody has cited it.

  • 1 Crossref citation — against 315 for the PNAS evaluation she is one of 295 authors on
  • Published in a Health Security supplement, licensed CC BY per Crossref metadata
  • No abstract is deposited with PubMed or Crossref, and the publisher blocks automated access — so this plate describes the paper’s framing from its title, venue and authorship, not from its text. I have not read it.

Plate XVII 2023 · a model

Evaluation of the US COVID-19 Scenario Modeling Hub for informing pandemic response under uncertainty

E. Howerton, L. Contamin, L. C. Mullany and 80 others including S. Wilson · Nature Communications 14:7260 · 10.1038/s41467-023-42680-x

Forecasting far ahead is impossible; projecting conditionally far ahead might not be. This paper tests that claim against nearly 1.8 million projections and separates two different ways of being wrong — a bad model, and a scenario the world declined to follow.

  • Nearly 1.8 million national and state-level projections since December 2020
  • Scenarios “remained close to reality for 22 weeks on average before the arrival of unanticipated SARS-CoV-2 variants invalidated key assumptions”
  • An ensemble “that preserved variation between models (using the linear opinion pool method) was consistently more reliable than any single model” while assumptions held
  • Projection interval coverage was near target levels — unlike the case forecasts in Plate XVIII
  • 83 authors · CC BY 4.0

Plate XVIII 2024 · a model

Challenges of COVID-19 Case Forecasting in the US, 2020–2021

V. K. Lopez, E. Y. Cramer, R. Pagano and 106 others including S. Wilson · PLOS Computational Biology 20(5):e1011200 · 10.1371/journal.pcbi.1011200

The most self-critical paper in the record, and the last COVID paper in it. Cases were supposed to be the leading indicator — the thing that would give decision-makers warning. The evaluation finds they were least reliable precisely when they mattered most.

  • Approximately 9.7 million forecasts of weekly state-level cases, from 24 teams, August 2020 – December 2021, at horizons of 1–4 weeks
  • Scored by weighted interval score with a Gaussian GEE model, split by epidemic phase using the effective reproduction number
  • Ensemble-based forecasts again outperformed other approaches; skill was higher for larger jurisdictions
  • 95% prediction interval coverage dropped below 50% during the growth phases of the winter 2020, Delta and Omicron waves
  • Closing recommendation: “it is critical for forecast users to appreciate current limitations and use a broad set of indicators”

Plate XIX 2018 · a mathematician

The Mathematically Gifted and Black Website

E. Graham, R. Higgins, C. Price, S. Wilson · Notices of the American Mathematical Society 65(2) · 10.1090/noti1633

Four mathematicians — Erica Graham, Raegan Higgins, Candice Price and Shelby Wilson — launched Mathematically Gifted and Black in 2017 and wrote it up in the Notices the following year. Each honoree answers the same short set of questions in their own words: where are you from, what got you interested, what are you proudest of.

For kids · ages 9–13

The class average doesn’t have a favourite colour

Nobody in your class is 11.4 years old with 1.8 pets.

If you add up everyone’s age and divide, you get a number that describes the class and belongs to nobody. That’s an average. Averages are useful — but they delete people.

Shelby Wilson is a mathematician who spends her whole career refusing to let that happen. When she studied cancer drugs, she didn’t build one model for “a mouse” — she built a model for each of 105 mice. When she studied grooming in animal groups, she found something surprising: the group with the three most popular animals — the ones everybody groomed — was the group that stayed sickest. The group where everyone groomed randomly got better.

Group activity — the vanishing classroom

  1. Everyone writes down three numbers about themselves: height in cm, hours slept last night, number of letters in their first name.
  2. Work out the class average for each. Write “The Average Student” on the board with those three numbers.
  3. Now go round the room: does anyone match all three? Usually nobody does.
  4. Plot everyone’s three numbers as dots. Draw the average as a big dot in the middle.
  5. Discuss: if you had to buy one chair for The Average Student, would it fit anybody? When is an average a good idea — and when is it a way of not looking?

For peers · seminar

One estimator, six substrates

The claim: her career is a single methodological commitment applied at ascending scales.

Wilson’s work is usually described by domain — immunology, oncology, sleep, behavioural ecology, epidemic forecasting. Sorted that way it reads as itinerant. Sorted by what unit carries the random effect, it is monotone: a cell (2010–13), a mouse (2013–15), a sleeper (2015–20), an animal in a troop (2018–20), a model (2021–24), a mathematician (2016–).

The load-bearing observation is that the mixed-effect model and the linear opinion pool are the same commitment run in opposite directions. One decomposes a population into shared plus individual structure; the other composes individuals into a population without collapsing their variance. Both refuse the point estimate. Both appear in her record, twelve years apart, in fields that do not cite each other.

Group activity — the isomorphism test

  1. Take three of her papers that look least related — say the 2013 Treg switching paper, the 2020 allogrooming paper, and the 2023 Scenario Modeling Hub evaluation.
  2. Write each on a card in three slots only: what is the population · what is the individual · what is being refused.
  3. Compare cards. If the slots fill the same way across unrelated fields, that is a real structural claim rather than a nice sentence.
  4. Then adversarially: find the paper that breaks it. Argue for the NTRU undergraduate thesis, or the multi-scale VEGF model, as genuine counterexamples.
  5. Repeat the exercise on a researcher in your own department whose CV looks scattered. Does an axis exist that makes it monotone?

For the public

Why the pandemic forecast was a committee

During COVID-19 the number you saw on the news was not one scientist’s guess. It was dozens, deliberately kept apart and then added up.

More than ninety research groups submitted forecasts to a public archive every week. Every submission was timestamped and kept, including the wrong ones. When researchers later scored them, the combination of all those forecasts was more consistently accurate than any single group — for eighteen months straight.

Two things are worth knowing. First: the combination works because it keeps the disagreement. The width of the shaded band on those charts is how much the modellers disagreed, and it is information, not embarrassment. Second: they published their failures. When forecasts of new cases were wrong, they said how wrong — during three big waves, ranges advertised as right 19 times out of 20 were right fewer than 1 time in 2.

Group activity — the jar and the crowd

  1. Fill a jar with sweets, marbles, anything countable. Only one person knows the true number.
  2. Everyone writes a private guess and a range they’re 90% sure contains the answer. No discussion.
  3. Collect all guesses. Compute the median. Compare it with each individual guess.
  4. Count how many people’s 90% ranges actually contained the truth. If far fewer than 90% did, you have just reproduced the paper’s central finding: people’s ranges are too narrow.
  5. Now let everyone talk for two minutes and guess again. Does the crowd get better — or just more confident? That difference is the whole problem.

Coda

She already said all this, in a title, in 2019

Everything above is my reading. But there is one item in her record that states the theory more compactly than this whole page does, and she wrote it. In Spring 2019 she returned to Spelman College — where she had taken two degrees, summa cum laude, in mathematics and computer science — to give the Etta Z. Falconer Lecture. This was the title.

Noether, Falconer, Mirzakhani, Kovalevsky, & Me

Etta Z. Falconer Lecture · Spelman College · Spring 2019

Not “women in mathematics”. Not “representation in the mathematical sciences”. Five names, one of them her own. It is precisely the operation she performs on 105 mice, on ninety forecasting teams, and on a population of T cells that refuses to be summarised: the category is replaced by its members, and the members are named.

And then the fact that closes the loop. Asked, on the website she co-founded, what she was proudest of, she answered:

“By far, my most proud accomplishment in my mathematical career was giving the Etta Z. Falconer Lecture at Spelman College in Spring 2019. Spelman is my beloved alma mater and Etta Falconer is my grandmother (and was a mathematician). Feeling like I had fulfilled her dream of forging the path for black women mathematicians was a feeling of generational accomplishment.” Shelby Wilson, Mathematically Gifted and Black, Black History Month 2022 honoree profile

Etta Zuber Falconer was among the first African-American women to earn a doctorate in mathematics, and spent her career at Spelman building the pipeline she is now commemorated by. The second name in that lecture title is not a historical reference. It is a family member, and it is the reason the fifth name belongs in the list.

Which is the last thing worth saying about the theory. The refusal to let a population average stand in for its members is not, in her case, a methodological preference she arrived at in graduate school. It is inherited. She learned it at the level where it matters most — a discipline that could have counted her as a percentage — and then spent twenty years proving it holds for cells, for mice, for sleepers, for troops, and for models too.
