Interlude — the keystone

The paper that says the quiet part out loud

Gray Roncal* & Dyer* (equal contribution), Gürsoy, Kording & Kasthuri · “From sample to knowledge: Towards an integrated approach for neuroscience discovery” · arXiv, 2016

The playbook for this page says a keystone is usually “one paper that states the theory most nakedly — often barely cited.” Here it is, recovered from the newly self-hosted PDFs: a 2016 position paper, written at the exact hinge between the career’s descent and its ascent, arguing that acquisition, processing and analysis must be optimized together, against the knowledge objective — because per-stage surrogate metrics (image quality, pixel accuracy, an arbitrary F1 operating point) “may not map smoothly to the overall objective of interest.”

  • Law one is in it: graphs and spatial point processes named as the knowledge representations of neuroanatomy — for “neurocartographers,” the same coinage as his dissertation title.
  • Law two is in it: choose metrics from the knowledge you want, then feed them backwards through every stage — the argument NRI, BENCHMARK, the human-baseline framework, and eventually “Show Your Work” each re-make in a new domain.
  • Even the later scale of ambition is in it: a worked case study chaining X-ray microtomography into electron microscopy on the same sample — the exact multimodal design the standards papers formalized eight years later.
  • Replace “imaging pipeline” with “classroom” and the argument reads as the learning-engineering thesis: judge the system by demonstrated capability, not by each stage’s proxy score.
