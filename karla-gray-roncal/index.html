The unified theory of —

Karla
Gray‑Roncal

An electrical engineer spends twenty-two years at a defence laboratory measuring how well people operate machines. Then she goes to medical school and becomes a multiple sclerosis neurologist. That looks like two careers. It is one job. Every time a person meets a system that is judging them — a sonar console, a benchmark, a clinical trial, a college application — she instruments the interface, and finds the instrument was wrong about the person.

The theory — three laws the bibliography obeys

She has never once blamed the person being measured.

Take the three works in this record that look least related — a 2008 submarine sonar study, a 2022 machine-learning benchmark paper, and a 2021 paper about race and multiple sclerosis — and write each one out as who is being measured / by what instrument / who gets misread when the instrument is wrong. The three slots fill the same way every time. That is not a theme. That is a method, and it has been running since 2003.

Law I

When a person appears to fail a system, instrument the system.

The reflex everywhere else in science is to treat a low score as a fact about the performer. Her reflex is to treat it as a fact about the measurement, and then go build a better one. This is the load-bearing law: it is why the submarine work and the neurology work are the same work.

It cuts both ways, which is what makes it a method rather than an advocacy position. Her framework paper is equally worried about human baselines that are too flattering — a human who brings outside knowledge to a task will make an algorithm look worse than it is.

Evidence. The 2022 Scientific Reports framework exists because human baselines in ML papers are built carelessly: it points out that the paper titled “Surpassing human-level performance on ImageNet classification” rested its claim on one human annotator. Failing to control for human memory, the authors write, “may result in artificially low performance measures for human participants.” — and in 2008 her job was writing the quantitative test measures that had been missing for submarine watch standers.
Law II

An outcome nobody schedules is an outcome nobody sees.

Detection, in her record, is never passive. Nothing is assumed to arrive on its own: not a relapse, not a symptom, not an insult, not a student. Somebody has to decide in advance to go and ask, and the asking has to be systematic, and then the thing that was supposedly rare turns out not to be.

This is the law with the cleanest number attached to it, and the number is embarrassing for the field: it says that a large share of what randomised MS trials record as “no relapse” is really “nobody asked.”

Evidence. In 165 participants in the VIDAMS trial, the odds of detecting a confirmed relapse in the two weeks after a scheduled coordinator check-in, versus the two weeks before, were 2.93× higher (95% CI 1.30–6.60, p = 0.01). The intervention is a phone call with specific questions on it.
Law III

Measure the obvious explanation properly, so that what survives it is real.

The lazy version of a disparities paper reports a gap. The lazy rebuttal says the gap is really about money. Her 2021 paper does the harder thing: it measures the money — four separate ways — puts it in the model, and then reports what is still standing on the other side.

That is the same move as adjusting a human baseline for memory and language before comparing it to an algorithm. Control for the explanation you already believe, and the residual becomes evidence instead of an argument.

Evidence. Across 1,214 Black American and 7,530 White American participants in the MS PATHS cohort, with education, employment and insurance carried as covariates, Black Americans had 1.47× the odds of severe versus mild disability (95% CI 1.18–1.86), scored 5.06 fewer correct on processing speed (95% CI 4.41–5.72), walked 0.66 s slower (95% CI 0.36–0.96), and had 0.77 mL less thalamic volume (95% CI 0.64–0.91). A separate within-race analysis used 9-digit ZIP codes to reach neighbourhood-level socioeconomic status — a deliberately harder instrument than asking.

Where the theory strains

One publication does not fit and we are not going to pretend it does. A 2015 review written as a Georgetown medical student — on the risk of gonadal failure in children treated for blood cancer — is a survivorship and fertility review. You can force it into the frame by calling late effects an unmeasured outcome, and that reading is not absurd, but it is a stretch and a stretch is worth admitting.

The rest of the record fits without being bent, including the parts written under a different surname, in a different discipline, seventeen years apart.

The story — seven acts, in the order the spine implies

One job, told from the bottom of the ladder.

Told chronologically this is a career-change story: engineer becomes doctor. Told along the axis, it is not a change at all. It is one method being carried, deliberately, closer and closer to the person it is about.

Act IRung 1 · a console

The sailors were not slow

She arrives at the Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory in 2003 with an electrical engineering degree, and is put on submarines. The Navy is pushing new sonar processing onto boats faster than anyone can say whether it helps. The question sounds technical and isn't: how do you tell whether a new system made the watch section better?

You cannot answer it by testing the software, because the software does not stand a watch. You have to measure the operator and the machine as one object. Her contribution to Watch Section Task Analysis (2008) and APB Technologies (2010) is the test measures themselves — the quantitative and qualitative instruments for assessing a crew and a console together. By 2010 she is running the introduction of Operator Machine Interfaces into the Navy's development process, which is to say: making new capability prove itself against the person who has to use it.

The first two entries in this bibliography cannot be read by anyone. The Submarine Technology Symposium is classified; there is no DOI and there never will be. A career about making performance measurable begins in a room where the results are not.

Act IIRung 2 · a ward

The same method, pointed at a hospital

In 2011 she runs a task analysis of intensive care units at Johns Hopkins. This is the hinge of the whole career and it is almost invisible, because it produced a mockup room rather than a paper. She takes the submarine methodology — watch the operators, map the task, find the seams where technology and people fail to meet — and points it at an ICU.

Then, in 2015, the most literal artefact in the record: asked to help engineers solve pressure ulcers, she finds they cannot read the clinical literature, and writes them a medical lexicon. A glossary. A translation layer between two professions that cannot yet hear each other. Eight years before she finishes medical school, the interface she is engineering is already the one between engineering and medicine.

This rung also holds the one publication that does not fit — a 2015 review of gonadal failure in children treated for blood cancer, written as a Georgetown student, which is a survivorship paper and not a measurement paper. It is here because it was written, not because it helps.

Act IIIRung 3 · a benchmark

One annotator

By 2018 she is leading the human–machine baseline task for IARPA's MICrONS programme — an effort to close the gap between human analysts and automated pattern recognition by reverse-engineering the brain's own algorithms, from a densely mapped cubic millimetre of cortex containing roughly a hundred thousand neurons. Her job in it is the unglamorous half: not building the algorithm, but making sure the humans it would be measured against had been measured properly.

That work becomes Cowley et al., Sci Rep 2022 ↗, and it is the keystone of this page. The field routinely announces that an algorithm has beaten people without reporting how many people, who they were, or how they were recruited. The paper whose title claims to be “surpassing human-level performance” on ImageNet rested that claim on one human annotator.

Their own demonstration is the tell. Given five examples, a ResNet-18 scored 92.7% and humans 94.6% — comparable. Given one example, the algorithm collapsed to 54.4% while the humans went to 95.8%, slightly up. But the framework's discipline is that it cuts both ways: it warns just as hard about human baselines that are too flattering, where a person's outside knowledge makes the machine look worse than it is. The point was never that humans win.

The same year and the same rung, aimed at a different population: the College Prep Program at APL, which she co-founded in 2009 and ran for eleven years.

Gray-Roncal et al., IEEE ISEC 2018 ↗ is the programme written up, and she is first and corresponding author on it, with a second affiliation to her own Preparation Meets Opportunity Foundation. Its premise is a measurement claim, not a charitable one: there exist students with the potential to excel who will not be identified, because the instruments that select for college are also quietly measuring mentoring and resources. Over 220 students mentored; nearly all on track to a degree. The follow-ups take on the scaling problem — mentorship at a distance, 2019 ↗ — and it is worth sitting with the fact that a submarine human-factors engineer and a volunteer college programme are, structurally, the same paper.

Act IVRung 4 · a test in the patient’s own hands

The gap that survived the explanation

She is a medical student now, and also still the department's engineer — her CV records that during 2017–2019 she was simultaneously doing clinical research on MS disparities and “providing engineering support for upcoming technology-based clinical research studies.” Both jobs, same building, same person.

The engineering job produces the PROSe headache app pilot (ANA 2018), tested on children with chronic headache. Note what it measures: not headache, but whether patients will use the instrument at all. An instrument nobody uses records nothing.

The research job produces Gray-Roncal et al., Neurology 2021 ↗, her most-cited work, first presented as an abstract in 2018. Across 8,744 people with MS, Black Americans had worse disability, worse scores on every objective test, more lesion volume and less grey matter than White Americans. The instruments are a tablet the patient operates themselves: a walking test, a nine-hole-peg analogue, a processing-speed test.

What makes it her paper rather than one more disparities paper is the middle move. It measures the obvious explanation — education, employment, insurance, and then, harder, neighbourhood deprivation reached through 9-digit ZIP codes — puts it in the model, and reports what is left. What is left is 1.47× the odds of severe disability, 5.06 fewer correct on processing speed, 0.77 mL less thalamus.

And then the finding inside the finding: lower neighbourhood status tracked worse scores among White Americans. Among Black Americans, that association was much less clear. Money bought one group a better outcome and did not appear to buy the other the same one.

One detail makes the volumetric result bite, and it comes from a paper she cites. In healthy cohorts, Black Americans have greater brain volumes than White Americans. In MS the sign flips. The deficit she measures is not a baseline difference being rediscovered; it is the disease landing differently.

Act VRung 5 · the body itself

Ask the tissue instead

MS monitoring means repeated MRI on a schedule nobody has individualised — expensive, burdensome, and set by expert opinion. With 1,131 patients from the Hopkins MS Center, the lesion-prediction algorithm ↗ asks who will actually show something new on their next scan: AUC 0.80, sensitivity 0.72, specificity 0.75, identifying 772 people who could safely wait longer.

The design choice is the interesting part. The threshold is deliberately tuned toward sensitivity — toward not missing lesions — rather than toward looking accurate. That is a human-factors decision about what a false negative costs a person, made inside a machine-learning paper. Her neurofilament light chain work (CMSC and ANA 2025) pushes the same idea further: a protein that leaks into blood when nerve fibres are damaged, checked against a person's own baseline, might flag damage before the scan does.

Act VIRung 6 · a question nobody had asked

Building the instrument that did not exist

Her 2021 paper ends by saying the residual points past socioeconomic status toward “other societal constructs (e.g., systemic racism).” The standard objection to that sentence is that you cannot measure such a thing, so the sentence is a gesture.

Gray-Roncal, Freeman & Mowry, ACTRIMS Forum 2025 is her answer, and the answer is a verb. The title is “Identifying racist microaggressions in health system encounters with people participating in neurology care and research.” Having found a gap that survived every instrument she had, she went and built the missing one.

The same rung holds Toward a Framework for Providing Equitable Opportunities to Activate STEM Talent (2024) ↗, fifteen years of the College Prep Program written up as a systems approach. The operative word is activate: the talent is modelled as already present and unexpressed, and the thing being designed is the opportunity that would reveal it. That is the same sentence as the machine learning paper, addressed to a different room.

Act VIIRung 7 · a conversation

Somebody has to call

Twenty-three years after she started writing test measures for sonar consoles, the instrument at the top of the ladder is a person with a list of questions and a phone.

Gray-Roncal et al., Mult Scler 2026 ↗ — she is first author — looks at the VIDAMS vitamin D trial, where coordinators contacted the 165 participants between visits to ask specific questions about symptoms. The odds of a confirmed relapse being detected in the two weeks after that contact, versus the two weeks before, were 2.93× higher (95% CI 1.3–6.60, p = 0.01).

Nothing biological happens at the moment of a phone call. The relapses in the “before” windows were happening too, and going uncounted — and confirmed relapse is the endpoint that phase 3 MS trials are powered on, the number that decides whether a drug works. She has spent a career on measurement, and the finding she puts her own name first on is that the field's most expensive instrument is leaking, and the patch is a scheduled conversation.

The fellowship funding this work is named for Sylvia Lawry, who in 1945 placed a classified advertisement in a newspaper asking anyone who had recovered from multiple sclerosis to write to her, and who founded the National MS Society on the fifty replies. The field began with somebody deciding to ask a question systematically.

Plate VII2022

Rung 3 · a benchmark · keystone

A framework for rigorous evaluation of human performance in human and machine learning comparison studies

The keystone. Machine learning papers routinely claim to beat people without saying how many people, who they were, or how they were recruited — the paper titled “Surpassing human-level performance on ImageNet classification” rested that claim on one annotator. The framework sets out how to build a human baseline that means something: control for memory, language and prior experience, match the trials, and run it like a psychology study, ethics review included.

134 participants · 200 matched trials · 45% easy / 45% hard / 10% attention checks. Drop from five examples to one and the algorithm falls 92.7% → 54.4%; the humans go 94.6% → 95.8%. But it cuts both ways — the paper warns equally about baselines that flatter humans and make an algorithm look worse than it is.

Handouts — three audiences, three activities

The same idea, told three ways.

One page each, designed to be printed and handed out. The claim underneath all three is identical: before you decide something about a person, check the thing you measured them with.

For kids · ages 8–12

The ruler with the end snapped off

Imagine measuring your whole class with a ruler that's missing its first three centimetres, and nobody notices. Everyone comes out three centimetres shorter than they really are. If you only look at the numbers, you'd think it was a very short class. The class is fine. The ruler is broken.

There's a scientist named Karla who does this for a living. First she checked the machines on submarines, to see whether the sailors were really making mistakes or whether the screens were confusing. Then she went to school to become a brain doctor and did exactly the same thing — except the ruler was the way doctors decide how sick somebody is.

Activity — the unfair test

  1. Two teams. Everyone will be timed naming ten animals.
  2. Secretly, give Team B a list written in tiny, blurry handwriting.
  3. Time everyone. Write the times on the board. Team B looks slower.
  4. Now ask the room: “Is Team B slower at animals?”
  5. Show them the two lists. Ask again.

The measurement said something about the people. It was really saying something about the paper.

For peers · clinicians & engineers

Your outcome measure is an intervention

The methodological claim in this record is uncomfortable and well evidenced: passive ascertainment does not merely add noise, it biases downward, and the bias is not distributed at random across your participants.

In the VIDAMS trial, the odds of catching a confirmed relapse were 2.93× higher in the fortnight after a scheduled coordinator contact than the fortnight before it (95% CI 1.30–6.60, p = 0.01). The contact was not treatment. It was somebody asking specific questions on a schedule — and it moved the primary endpoint.

The parallel in ML is exact. A human baseline assembled without controlling for memory, language and fatigue produces “artificially low performance measures for human participants,” and every algorithm compared against it inherits the error.

Activity — the ascertainment audit

Bring one live protocol or benchmark to the table. In pairs, twenty minutes:

  1. Name your primary outcome. Now say, out loud, the exact mechanism by which it reaches your database.
  2. Who has to act for it to be recorded? A participant deciding to call? A clinician noticing?
  3. Which participants are least likely to take that action — and is that group also your comparison of interest?
  4. Design the cheapest scheduled prompt that would remove the dependence. Estimate what it would cost.

If nobody has to act, you have a measurement. If somebody does, you have a behaviour — and you are measuring that instead.

For everyone · patients & the public

“Nobody asked” is not the same as “nothing happened”

A lot of medicine runs on you noticing something and deciding it's worth a phone call. That sounds neutral. It isn't. It quietly measures how easy it is for you to call, how sure you feel that you'll be believed, and whether you can afford to be wrong about it.

When researchers started ringing multiple sclerosis trial participants on a schedule and asking specific questions, they found nearly three times as many confirmed relapses. Those relapses had been happening the whole time.

The same logic runs through the disparities work. Black Americans with MS in one large study had more disability and more visible damage on brain scans — and the study measured income, education, employment, insurance and even neighbourhood first, precisely so that nobody could wave the finding away.

Activity — the appointment you actually get

For a family, a support group, or a waiting room. Take ten minutes together:

  1. Each person writes down one symptom or worry they had in the last year and did not report to a doctor.
  2. Next to it, write why not. (“Seemed small.” “Didn't want to be a bother.” “No appointment for six weeks.”)
  3. Read only the reasons aloud, anonymously. Notice how few are about the symptom.
  4. Pick one. Put it on the agenda for the next visit, in writing, before you go.

You are allowed to be the scheduled contact.

Coda

Her own version.

The honest finding first: we could not locate a single sentence, anywhere in public, in which Karla Gray-Roncal states a through-line for her own work. No manifesto, no essay, no lab-page paragraph. The closest thing that exists is four lines under the heading Research Focus in her curriculum vitae.

Research focus — her words, verbatim, from her CV
  • Clinical trials addressing detection of relapses in MS and use of biomarkers for disease monitoring
  • Technology integration in neurological care and research practices
  • Healthcare disparities and access in multiple sclerosis
  • Mentoring and education of next generation trailblazers and leaders

Read as a list, that is four jobs, which is exactly how a CV is built to read. Read as one sentence, it is: detect what is being missed, build the instrument that detects it, check who the instrument is missing, and go find the people it never counted.

One word in that list is worth following. Trailblazers appears in her research focus; it appears in the title of her 2018 paper about underserved students; and it appears again in the 2024 framework, which defines trailblazing students as those with both high potential and significant barriers. It is her word, used the same way across seventeen years and two disciplines — a person whose ability is real and whose obstacles are also real, and whom the standard instrument will therefore score wrong.

She holds the Sylvia Lawry Physician Fellowship. Sylvia Lawry founded the National Multiple Sclerosis Society in 1946 after placing a classified advertisement in a newspaper the year before, asking anyone who had recovered from multiple sclerosis to write to her. Around fifty people replied. None had recovered. The field she now works in was founded by somebody deciding to ask a question systematically and count the answers — and the paper that carries her own name first says the same thing, seventy-eight years later, with a confidence interval attached.
